What Headroom needs from Jabil, and why

Headroom is the proposed advanced Credit Management Dashboard for Jabil. This document is the data request: the SAP data we would need, the outside data we would add, how the two connect, and the decisions only Jabil can make.

  • Project IND2620-0011
  • NUS MSc capstone, 23 weeks
  • Starts 11 January 2027
  • Version 1.0
Headroom Data requirements

Read this in 60 seconds

Three answers carry the rest of the document. Everything after this box is the detail behind them.

What we need from SAP

Invoice-level AR with due dates and paid dates, about three years back; open customer orders; dedicated stock; customer master and hierarchy; and credit-limit history. Read-only, on a schedule, with masked names if Jabil prefers.

What we add from outside

Public filings, legal-entity identity, news, share prices and sector data. Free sources first. Only public identifiers leave Jabil's network: a ticker, a CIK, an LEI or a company name that is already on a public register.

What only Jabil can decide

Which data we may use, the rules for public sources and language models, where the tool runs, and who owns handover. The tool advises; a Jabil analyst decides. Nothing is ever written back to SAP.

No claim is made here that Jabil has given us any data. Every row in this document is a request, and any table or field name from Jabil's system is marked as indicative until an SAP colleague confirms it.

The gap this project fills

Jabil's current Credit Management Dashboard shows exposure as it is today: accounts receivable, dedicated inventory, open purchase orders and credit limits from SAP, at customer-code and customer-hierarchy level. It is a faithful picture of the position, and it is always a picture of the past. It cannot say that a limit will be breached in nine weeks, that a customer's payment behaviour has quietly changed, or that a group has just filed something material. Headroom predicts those things, and shows its reasons.

The difference between reporting and predicting is data. This document sets out exactly what we would need, what we would do with it, and what we would do if a piece of it is not available.

What this document does not ask for

It does not ask for a change to any live SAP system. It does not ask for a user that can write, post or change anything. It does not ask for access to customer contact details or to any commercial terms beyond the credit limit itself. It does not ask Jabil to buy anything before the prototype has shown that it works.

What the priority column means

  • Required — the forecast or the score cannot run without it. If a required row is missing, that module does not ship.
  • Needed — the score still runs, but it is weaker and harder to explain, and the back-test has less to work with.
  • Nice to have — improves detail and presentation only. We would not ask Jabil to build anything for it.

The shape of the extract we would ask for

One row per document or item, a stable key that survives a system change, and a timestamp for when the row last changed. Names can be masked: a stable customer key plus a masked name is enough for the models, and the analyst's screen can show whatever the CMD already shows. Where a field is genuinely absent, monthly totals are a workable fallback — we would say so on screen rather than pretend the detail exists.

One request, not nine

We would rather receive one well-defined extract covering these rows than nine separate reports. The refresh table in the How it connects tab shows how often each part is read; the SAP extract itself is nightly, changed rows only.

External data: what we would add, and what it costs

SAP tells us how a customer is behaving with Jabil. It cannot tell us that the customer's own margins are falling, that its auditor has resigned, or that its sector has just been hit by a tariff. That is what the external catalogue is for. Five families of signal are involved: filings, events, news, market and sector.

Free first, paid only to fill gaps

Everything the prototype needs is available free: SEC EDGAR, GLEIF, GDELT, FRED, the World Bank and openFDA. We would build and prove the radar on those before asking Jabil to consider anything paid. Where a paid source is listed below, it is listed because a free one may not cover it — and the first question is always whether Jabil already holds a licence we may reuse.

How a customer is matched to an outside source

External data is only useful if it lands on the right company. Every customer is placed in one of three tiers, and the tier decides which signals can apply. Matching is done on public identifiers only — never on amounts.

Three tiers
Coverage figures are illustrative, taken from the team's own sample of a 120-group portfolio. They are not Jabil data.
Tier Who is in it Signal families that apply How we match it Illustrative coverage
Tier A Listed, and files with the SEC All five: filings, events, news, market and sector. Ticker or LEI to CIK, confirmed against the registered name. 49 of 120 groups
Tier B Listed elsewhere, no SEC filing Market where a price is available, news, and sector. LEI, ISIN, or name with country and registered address. 31 of 120 groups
Tier C Private company or a subsidiary News and sector, plus the parent's filings where LEI data names a parent. LEI, or name and address; the parent's LEI where it exists. 40 of 120 groups

Tier C is the honest limit of this approach. Roughly a third of a typical portfolio is private, and for those customers the radar is thinner: no filings, no market signal, and a greater reliance on payment behaviour from SAP. That is one reason the forecast and the score are built to work on SAP data alone.

How it connects

Seven questions come up whenever this project meets an SAP or IT team. They are answered below in the order they are usually asked, with a diagram and the two tables that matter: what refreshes when, and what a normal day looks like.

    1. Can it link to SAP, and how?

    Yes, and there are three realistic read-only routes. All three are in normal use at manufacturers of Jabil's size, and we would take whichever Jabil prefers. None of them requires a change to how SAP works today.

    (a) Through Jabil's existing data platform, BW or CDS views

    If the current CMD already draws receivables, orders, stock and limits from a governed layer, we would read from that same layer. This is the least new work for IT, it reuses authorisations and definitions that already exist, and the figures match what the credit team sees today. The trade-off is that the layer may not expose the item-level history we need, so a change request would go into the platform team's queue.

    (b) An OData service exposed by SAP Gateway

    SAP publishes a read-only service and we call it on a schedule. Nothing to copy, no files to manage, and the data is close to real time. The trade-offs: the service has to be built and released by Jabil, and pulling three years of history through a service is slower and heavier than reading a file.

    (c) A scheduled file drop

    Jabil runs a query or a report on a schedule and writes CSV or Parquet to a landing folder we can read. This is usually the easiest option to approve, needs no service development, and is simple to audit because every run leaves a dated file. The trade-off is that it is batch only — typically a day behind — and the extract definition has to be maintained.

    We only ever read

    In all three cases the connection is read-only. We never write to SAP, never post a document, and never need a user with change authority. A dedicated read-only account, or an extract run under an existing one, is all we would ask for. Customer names can be masked in the extract: a stable key and a masked name are enough for the models.

    2. What actually leaves Jabil's network?

    Nothing that identifies a customer. The only outbound requests are to public sources, and the only values in those requests are public identifiers: a ticker, a CIK number, an LEI, or a company name that is already on a public register. Customer codes, names, addresses, invoice numbers, amounts and limits stay inside the network.

    Where we score text, the language model runs locally, so the text itself is not sent anywhere. Every outbound call is logged with the identifier it used, which means the claim can be checked rather than believed.

    3. Where does it run?

    On one virtual machine, or in one slot on Jabil's container platform, inside the network. One Docker container runs the pipeline; a second serves the screens. PostgreSQL holds four schemas — raw, clean, signals and results — so that any figure on a screen can be traced back to the row it came from. Python runs the pipeline: extract, match, features, models. The screens are a small React web app served from the same host, or Power BI if Jabil would rather keep reporting in the existing stack. Nothing is exposed to the internet, and analysts reach it on the internal network.

    6. The pipeline stages

    Five stages, in this order, every day. Each stage keeps its output, so any figure on a screen can be followed back to the row it came from.

    Stage 1

    Raw

    Exactly what arrived, with the file or the response and the time it arrived. Kept for audit and so that any day can be re-run without asking SAP again.

    Stage 2

    Clean

    Typed, de-duplicated, checked against a rule set, and matched to the right legal entity. Rows that fail are quarantined with a reason, not dropped silently.

    Stage 3

    Signals

    One row per customer per day: the measures the models read, such as DPD buckets, payment delay, order momentum, limit headroom, news tone and the market signal.

    Stage 4

    Results

    Scores, forecasts, drivers and one suggested next step, written once per run so that any past run can be reproduced exactly.

    Stage 5

    Serve

    The screens and the small API they read. Read-only, always showing the latest completed run, with the run time printed on every page.

    7. Orchestration

    An orchestrator runs the schedule, rather than plain cron. Apache Airflow is the obvious choice and is free; if Jabil already runs a scheduler, we would use that instead and keep the same job definitions. Cron can start a job. It cannot tell you what ran, in what order, what it depended on, or why yesterday's numbers differ from today's — and those are exactly the questions an auditor asks.

    • Order — extract, then checks, then matching, then models, then publish. Nothing starts before what it depends on has finished.
    • Retries — a failed step retries on its own, and only fails the run after the retries are exhausted. A source being briefly unavailable does not cost the day's list.
    • Audit log — every run is recorded with its start, finish, inputs, outputs and row counts. That record is the evidence that yesterday's list was built from yesterday's data.
    • Re-runs — a single day, or a single customer, can be re-run without disturbing anything else, which is what makes a back-test honest.
